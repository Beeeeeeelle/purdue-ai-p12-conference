Research 1

Examining K–12 Teachers’ Perceptions of Artificial Intelligence

Jared S. Turney, Ph.D. Candidate; Assistant Professor of Education and Grant Coordinator, University of Evansville

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly becoming part of K–12 education, yet effective implementation requires more than understanding whether teachers perceive AI as useful. This quantitative correlational study examined relationships among teachers’ perceptions of AI for teaching, student learning, and ethical considerations. Findings showed a strong positive relationship between perceptions of AI for teaching and student learning, while ethical perceptions were not significantly related to either. The study highlights the need for professional development, leadership, and school policies that intentionally integrate instructional value, student learning, and ethical responsibility.

Research 2

Making Sense of AI: District Leaders' Perspectives on Organizational Change, Policy, and Practice

Lamonica Williams, Clinical Assistant Professor, Purdue University; Victoria Lowell, Clinical Professor, Purdue University

As AI becomes integrated into K–12 education, districts are adapting across teaching, learning, governance, and organizational practice. Guided by Sensemaking Theory, this mixed-methods study examines district leaders’ perceptions, implementation priorities, ethical considerations, policy development, stakeholder engagement, and decision-making. Findings indicate wide variation in district readiness, governance, support structures, and approaches to responsible, equitable, and sustainable implementation.

Research 3

Examining the Utility of Indiana School Districts’ AI Policies: A Qualitative Document Analysis

Francine Denecke, Assistant Principal, Harrison High School

This qualitative document analysis examined AI policies across Indiana’s 290 public K–12 local education agencies using criteria covering AI literacy, privacy and safety, access, ethics and transparency, monitoring, professional development, and family and community engagement. Although 125 agencies had adopted a policy, few provided comprehensive guidance. The findings show that policy adoption alone does not ensure clear direction for students, staff, and families.

Session 5STEW 278

AI Competency Starts with Language

Colin Swift, Eduba

AI Curriculum ApplicationsGrade levels P-2;3-5;6-8;9-12
View detailsHide details

Session description

Purdue now requires every incoming undergraduate to demonstrate AI competency to graduate. What about the teachers who prepare them? Most teachers use AI at work, but 82% say they have received no formal guidance on how—and students have noticed. Current frameworks and standards ask students to engage with, create with, manage, and shape AI; communicate and collaborate with it; and evaluate it for accuracy. How can educators confidently evaluate students’ AI-assisted work if they have not first learned to be AI-competent? In this workshop, participants will begin producing meaningful outputs and helping students demonstrate AI competencies by starting with the language they already use to teach. They will turn a real learning objective into just three words for an AI tool such as ChatGPT or Claude, run those three words twice as a prompt, and evaluate how the same three words can produce drastically different results.

Session 25STEW 278

Making AI in Education Responsible: Classroom-Ready Ethical Guidelines for Teachers and Students

Bokyung Go, Postdoctoral Fellow, Governance and Responsible AI Lab (GRAIL), Purdue University

AI Ethics and PolicyGrade levels P-2; 3-5; 6-8; 9-12
View detailsHide details

Session description

Teachers are increasingly told to use AI "responsibly," yet most available guidance states values rather than actions. This session presents 66 classroom-ready ethical guidelines for AI use, developed through a systematic review of AIED ethics literature (2014–2023), three rounds of expert validation using CVI and inter-rater agreement, and focus group interviews with practicing teachers and students. The underlying framework reinterprets eleven ethical principles — transparency, justice and fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility, privacy, beneficence, freedom and autonomy, trust, sustainability, dignity, and solidarity — for classroom conditions and positions teachers and students as the primary ethical actors. Each principle yields three teacher actions and three student actions mapped to the before, during, and after phases of AI use, so both the responsible actor and the moment of action are explicit. Participants will apply the guidelines to familiar scenarios, such as an AI writing tool flagging a multilingual student's dialect as incorrect, and consider how wording and scope must shift across grade bands. The session closes with open problems the guidelines do not yet resolve: generative AI, academic integrity, hallucinated content, and attribution. The full guideline set is openly available for immediate classroom use.
